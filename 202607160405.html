English Learning Content for Beginners

Topic: Writing Abstracts

Dialogue

Alice: Oh, Bob, you look like you've seen a ghost! What's up?

Bob: Alice! Worse than a ghost. I'm trying to write this abstract!

Alice: An abstract? Oh no, I remember those! They're tiny but mighty, right?

Bob: Mighty? More like 'mightily difficult'! It's supposed to be a summary, but how do I summarize 5,000 words into 150?

Alice: Haha, I know! It's like trying to fit an elephant into a shoebox.

Bob: Exactly! And my brain just freezes. What even *is* the main point of my paper anymore?

Alice: Don't worry! My old professor used to say, 'Imagine you're explaining your whole project to someone on a very short elevator ride.'

Bob: An elevator ride? So, 'Ding! My paper is about... *ding!* ...okay, I'm out of time!'?

Alice: Pretty much! You need to tell them the problem, what you did, what you found, and why it matters. Quick and clear!

Bob: Problem, did, found, matters... That's a good checklist! I always get stuck on 'what I did'.

Alice: Just the main method! Not every tiny step. Think 'how' you got your results, not 'every single ingredient in the cake recipe'.

Bob: Aha! Less ingredients, more 'delicious cake'. I like that. So, start with the 'why'?

Alice: Yes, the background. Why is your topic important? Then your main question or purpose.

Bob: Okay, I think I'm getting it. So, no big fancy words, right? Just clear language.

Alice: Exactly! An abstract is for everyone to understand quickly. Keep it simple, Bob.

Bob: Thanks, Alice! You've saved me from a mountain of stress. Now, where's that shoebox for my elephant?

Current Situation

Writing abstracts is a common task in academics, research, and professional reports. An abstract is a short summary of a longer work, like a research paper, thesis, or article. Its main purpose is to give readers a quick overview of the entire content, allowing them to decide if they want to read the full text. It's often the first thing people read, so it needs to be clear, concise, and informative. While it seems small, writing an effective abstract can be challenging because you need to condense complex information into a few sentences, highlighting the key points without unnecessary details.

Key Phrases

  • What's up?
    Example: "Hey, John, what's up? You look happy!"
    Meaning: A casual way to ask "How are you?" or "What's happening?"
  • Look like you've seen a ghost
    Example: "You're so pale! You look like you've seen a ghost."
    Meaning: To look very scared, shocked, or surprised.
  • Tiny but mighty
    Example: "This small car is tiny but mighty; it can go very fast."
    Meaning: Something that is small in size but very powerful, effective, or important.
  • Get stuck on something
    Example: "I always get stuck on the last math problem."
    Meaning: To be unable to make progress with something, to be unable to solve a problem or continue with a task.
  • Pretty much
    Example: "A: So, you finished all your homework? B: Pretty much, just one part left."
    Meaning: Almost entirely; largely; mostly.
  • Quick and clear
    Example: "Please give me quick and clear instructions."
    Meaning: Fast and easy to understand; without confusion or delay.
  • Save someone from something
    Example: "Thank you for saving me from that boring meeting!"
    Meaning: To help someone avoid a difficult, unpleasant, or dangerous situation.

Grammar Points

1. Present Simple Tense

We use the Present Simple to talk about general truths, facts, habits, and scheduled events. It describes things that are always true or happen regularly.

  • Structure: Subject + Verb (base form) / Verb + -s/-es (for he/she/it)
  • Examples from dialogue:
    • "It is supposed to be a summary." (General truth about abstracts)
    • "My brain just freezes." (A regular habit/reaction)
    • "An abstract is for everyone to understand quickly." (General fact)
  • Your turn:
    • I read books every day.
    • The sun rises in the east.

2. Modal Verbs (Need to, Should)

Modal verbs like "need to" and "should" express necessity or advice.

  • Need to: Expresses necessity or obligation. It means something is very important or required.
    • Structure: Subject + need to + base verb
    • Example from dialogue: "You need to tell them the problem..." (It is necessary to tell them)
    • Your turn: "I need to study for my exam."
  • Should: Expresses advice or recommendation. It means something is a good idea.
    • Structure: Subject + should + base verb
    • Example: "You should rest if you are tired." (It is a good idea to rest)

3. Comparatives

We use comparatives to compare two things or people. We add '-er' to short adjectives or use 'more' before longer adjectives, followed by 'than'.

  • Structure: Adjective + -er + than (for short adjectives like 'big', 'fast') OR More + Adjective + than (for long adjectives like 'difficult', 'beautiful')
  • Examples from dialogue:
    • "Worse than a ghost." (Comparing the situation to seeing a ghost)
    • "More like 'mightily difficult'!" (Comparing the abstract to something 'mightily difficult')
  • Your turn:
    • This book is more interesting than that movie.
    • My car is faster than yours.
