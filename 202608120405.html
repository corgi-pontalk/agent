English for Beginners: Scientific Writing (Sort Of!)

Dialogue

Alice: Hey Bob, you look stressed. Everything okay?

Bob: Alice! Oh, it's this "scientific writing" assignment. I don't know where to start!

Alice: Scientific writing? Are we suddenly becoming rocket scientists?

Bob: I know, right? I thought we were just studying history, not quantum physics!

Alice: Maybe it just means writing very, very neat paragraphs? With no scribbles?

Bob: Or using really big words that nobody understands. Like "interdisciplinary methodological framework"!

Alice: (Giggles) "Interdisciplinary methodological framework"! My brain hurts just thinking about it.

Bob: Mine too! Do we need to wear a lab coat while we write this? For authenticity?

Alice: And use a microscope to check our grammar? Every tiny comma?

Bob: Probably! My professor said it needs to be "clear and concise."

Alice: "Clear and concise"... but also "scientific." It's a paradox!

Bob: I guess it means absolutely no emojis in our report. Not even a single smiley face.

Alice: No emojis?! That's the hardest part! My conclusions will feel so lonely without a crying laughing face.

Bob: Exactly! Maybe we can just describe an experiment about how difficult scientific writing is for beginners.

Alice: That's genius, Bob! Let's start with that "hypothesis": Scientific writing makes brains melt. Experiment: commence!

Current Situation

Scientific writing might sound intimidating, but it's a very important skill! It's not just for scientists in labs; it's about communicating information clearly, accurately, and logically. You'll find it in school reports, university essays, research papers, and even professional emails or business proposals. For beginners, it means learning to present facts without personal opinions, using precise language, and structuring your ideas well. The goal is for your reader to understand exactly what you mean without confusion. It's a skill that develops over time, and even small steps, like organizing your thoughts before writing, are part of the journey!

Key Phrases

  • look stressed: To appear worried or anxious.
    • Example: You look stressed. Did you have a bad day?
  • where to start: How or from what point to begin something.
    • Example: I have so much homework, I don't know where to start.
  • I know, right?: An informal expression showing strong agreement.
    • Example: "This weather is beautiful!" – "I know, right?"
  • thinking about it: When you are considering something.
    • Example: My head hurts just thinking about it.
  • lab coat: A white coat worn by scientists or doctors in a laboratory or medical setting.
    • Example: Scientists often wear a lab coat to protect their clothes.
  • clear and concise: Easy to understand and brief, saying a lot in a few words.
    • Example: Your report needs to be clear and concise.
  • It's a paradox: A statement that seems to contradict itself but may nonetheless be true.
    • Example: It's a paradox; the more I learn, the more I realize I don't know.
  • no emojis: The absence of small digital images used to express ideas or emotions.
    • Example: For formal emails, please use no emojis.
  • That's genius!: An exclamation meaning "That's a brilliant idea!"
    • Example: You found a great solution! That's genius!
  • hypothesis: A proposed explanation made on the basis of limited evidence as a starting point for further investigation.
    • Example: We need to form a hypothesis before we begin our experiment.

Grammar Points

  1. Simple Present Tense

    We use the simple present tense to talk about habits, facts, general truths, and scheduled events.

    • Fact/General Truth: "My brain hurts." "It needs to be clear."
    • Habit/Routine: "I don't know where to start." (Implies a general state of not knowing.)

    Structure: Subject + Base Verb (add -s/-es for he/she/it)

    • I/You/We/They learn.
    • He/She/It learns.

  2. "To Be" Verb (am, is, are)

    The verb "to be" is used to describe states, identities, or characteristics.

    • Description: "You are stressed." "It is a paradox."
    • Identity: "We are friends."

    Forms:

    • I am
    • You/We/They are
    • He/She/It is

  3. Modal Verbs (can, need to, should)

    Modal verbs express ability, necessity, permission, or advice.

    • Can: Expresses ability or possibility. (e.g., "Maybe we can just describe...")
    • Need to: Expresses necessity or obligation. (e.g., "Do we need to wear a lab coat?")
    • Should: Gives advice or recommendation. (e.g., "You should practice writing.")

    Structure: Subject + Modal Verb + Base Verb

  4. "Don't know where to..."

    This phrase is used to express uncertainty about how to begin a specific action or task.

    • Example: "I don't know where to start with this project."
    • Example: "She doesn't know where to go next."

    • I _____ (not know) the answer to this question.
    • She _____ (need) to finish her report by tomorrow.

Answers

  1. Fill in the Blanks

    • He looks tired today.
    • This is a very interesting book.
    • I don't know the answer to this question.
    • She needs to finish her report by tomorrow.
    • We are ready for the exam.

  2. Match the phrase

    • 1. I know, right? → c) I completely agree with you.
    • 2. Where to start? → a) How do I begin this task?
    • 3. That's genius! → b) That's a very smart or brilliant idea.

  3. Create a sentence

    • You should write clearly. (or You can write clearly.)
    • I can help you with your homework.
    • We need to finish this assignment by dinner.

  4. Dialogue Completion

    • A: This new movie is absolutely amazing!
    • B: I know, right? I loved it too.
    • A: I have to write a long essay, but I feel so overwhelmed.
    • B: Oh no! I guess you don't know where to start?
